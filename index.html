BenfordGap

A first-digit fraud screen for any column of amounts. Paste a payables export, an expense list or a claims file. It counts how often each first digit 1 to 9 appears and compares that with Benford's Law, the pattern that genuine financial amounts usually follow. You get a verdict, the strength of the evidence, and the digits to look at first. It runs on your computer: nothing you paste is sent anywhere.

Start here

  1. Export one column of amounts from your accounts system: supplier payments, expense claims, refunds, journal amounts. Hundreds of rows is good; thousands is better.
  2. Paste it above, or open the CSV file. If it has a header row, pick the amount column. Amounts like £1,240.00, (85.50) or -99 are read as they are.
  3. Press Screen it. Read the verdict first, then the notes underneath: they say if rows were skipped or if the data is the wrong shape for this test.
  4. Look at the digits named "look here first". Those first digits turn up more often than the law expects. Filter your ledger to amounts starting with that digit and read them: round numbers, repeats, amounts just under an approval limit, one supplier.
  5. Keep the fingerprint. The result carries a SHA-256 fingerprint of the input and the result. Anyone with the same column gets the same fingerprint, so a reviewer can check your screen was run on what you say it was.

How it works

In many real collections of amounts (invoices, populations, river lengths, share prices) the first digit is 1 about 30% of the time and 9 under 5% of the time. That is Benford's Law: the share for digit d is log10(1 + 1/d). It holds when amounts arise from many independent causes and spread over several orders of magnitude. Invented amounts usually don't follow it, because people pick digits more evenly, and amounts pushed under a limit pile up on the limit's first digit.

The three tests, in plain words

  • MAD (mean absolute deviation): the average gap between the share you have for each digit and the share the law expects. Mark Nigrini, who brought Benford's Law into auditing, gives bands for first digits: up to 0.006 close, up to 0.012 acceptable, up to 0.015 marginal, above that nonconformity. MAD does not grow just because the file is big, so from 1,000 amounts up the verdict uses these bands. Below 1,000 they flag most honest files (see the measurement below), so they are shown but do not decide.
  • Chi-square: one number for how far all nine counts are from the expected counts. Above 15.507 the gap would happen by chance less than 5% of the time; above 20.09, less than 1%. It keeps its promise at small sizes: about 5 honest columns in 100 pass the 5% line, whatever the size. So under 1,000 amounts the verdict comes from chi-square: past the 1% line nonconformity, past the 5% line marginal, otherwise acceptable. On large files it flags small, harmless gaps, so from 1,000 up it is shown as supporting evidence, not the verdict.
  • z per digit: whether one digit on its own is too common or too rare. Above 1.96 is unusual for one digit (5% level), and the table marks it. Testing nine digits at once at that line would name a digit in about one honest column in five (1 − 0.9759 ≈ 20%), so a digit is named to "look here first" only above 2.773: the same 5% shared over the nine digits (the Bonferroni correction). What it can't tell you

  • It is a screen, not proof. A nonconforming column tells you where to look. It does not say anyone did anything wrong. A conforming column is not a clean bill of health either: a fraud that copies the normal pattern passes.
  • Some data never follows the law, and the tool cannot know that for you. Examples: assigned numbers (invoice numbers, phone numbers), prices set at fixed points (£9.99), amounts with a built-in cap or floor, a single fee charged many times. Use it on amounts that arise naturally.
  • It needs enough data. Below the "fewest amounts to judge" setting (100 by default) it gives no verdict. From 100 to 999 amounts the verdict comes from chi-square, which can only see large gaps at that size: a small pile-up can pass. From 1,000 up it uses Nigrini's MAD bands. If you lower the setting under 100, the result says the verdict is weak.
  • It needs spread. If the amounts span less than two orders of magnitude (say, all between £100 and £9,999), the verdict is still given, with a note underneath that it is weak. Benford's Law describes amounts that range over several powers of ten; a narrow band of similar amounts can't show the pattern.
  • First digits only. Nigrini's fuller method adds first-two-digit and last-two-digit tests, which this version does not run.
  • Number formats. Commas are read only as thousands separators (1,234.56). European formats like 1.234,56 are refused and counted as unreadable, never guessed.

What version 2.1 fixed

Version 2 passed its gate, and the same method found two more faults: running honest columns through it at each size.

  1. It took the verdict from Nigrini's MAD bands at every size. On honest columns of 100 amounts, those bands flag almost all of them (the table above shows the measured share). Version 2.1 uses chi-square under 1,000 amounts, where it keeps its 5% promise, and the MAD bands from 1,000 up.
  2. It named a digit to look at first whenever one digit passed z 1.96. With nine digits tested at once, that happens by chance in about one honest column in five (nine chances at 2.5% on the too-common side: 1 − 0.9759 ≈ 20%). Version 2.1 uses the nine-digit line, 2.773.

What version 2 fixed

Version 1 passed its own gate and still had three faults. Each was found by running a whole class of inputs, not by reading the code:

  1. It found the first digit by dividing by 10 in a loop, which drifts. 1,700 ordinary numbers got the wrong digit (3×1027 read as 2), while a comment said it had been checked exhaustively. Version 2 reads the digit from the number's decimal form, and the tests check the full range.
  2. It called three numbers "maximally divergent". Version 2 refuses to judge below a sample floor.
  3. It silently dropped every row it could not read, including £1,234.56. Version 2 reads ledger formats and reports every skipped row.

Risks, answered straight

What if it flags honest data?
It will sometimes. On columns that follow the law exactly it flags about 5 in 100, measured live in "How it works". Real honest data that doesn't follow the law is flagged more often (see "What it can't tell you"). That is why it names digits to look at rather than people to accuse. The cost of a false flag is a few minutes of filtering.
What if it misses fraud?
It can. A fraud that mimics the normal distribution, or a single large payment, won't show. Treat it as one control among several, not the only one.
Can I trust the code?
Read it: the kernel is under 300 lines, with the reason for each rule written next to it. Every line is covered by tests that a mutation gate checks. Version 1 passed its gate and still had faults, and so did version 2. Each was found by attacking it with a whole class of input, and each attack is now part of the tests.
Who is accountable for a decision made on it?
Your team. The tool screens; people decide.

The explainer

Why first digits are uneven. Take an amount that grows steadily, like a balance earning interest. It spends a long time with first digit 1 (from 100 to 199 it must double), and much less with first digit 9 (from 900 to 999 is only an 11% rise). When many amounts arise from growth, multiplication and mixing over several orders of magnitude, their first digits settle into the share log10(1 + 1/d): 30.1% ones, 17.6% twos, down to 4.6% nines. Simon Newcomb noticed it in 1881 from the worn pages of logarithm tables. Frank Benford tested it on over 20,000 numbers in 1938.

Why fraud shows. People inventing amounts tend to spread digits evenly and avoid repeating themselves. People working around a control push amounts just under it: claims of £4,950 to stay under a £5,000 approval, so digit 4 is over-represented. Both leave a mark in the first-digit counts that a simple, repeatable test can see.

Why these thresholds. Chi-square is the textbook test, but with thousands of rows it flags gaps too small to matter. Nigrini's MAD bands were set from real accounting data to separate "close enough" from "worth a look" on large files, and they do not grow with file size. But on small files honest data drifts further from the law by chance, and the fixed bands flag it. So each test is used where it holds: chi-square under 1,000 amounts, MAD from 1,000 up. Both, and the per-digit z, are always shown so you can see the evidence.

Why a fingerprint. A screen is only as good as the data it was run on. The SHA-256 fingerprint of the input column and the result lets anyone confirm, later, that the screen in a working paper was run on the column it claims.

FAQ

What happens with a huge file?
It runs in your browser's memory. Every result says how long the screen took on your machine, so you can see the cost for yourself; the honest-data measurement in "How it works" screened over a million amounts as this page loaded. For files too big to paste, use the command line.
What happens with bad rows?
They are counted, never guessed. The result says how many rows were unreadable and how many were zero, and leaves them out.
Do negative amounts count?
Yes, by their size: a refund of (85.50) counts as first digit 8. Screen refunds as their own column if you want to look at them apart.
Does it work offline?
Once the page has loaded, the screen needs no connection. For a permanent offline copy, download the repo and open index.html from a local web server, or use the command line.
Command line?
With Node.js installed: node cli.mjs payments.csv amount prints the same result as JSON. See the README.
